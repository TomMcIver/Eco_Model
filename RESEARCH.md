# Economic Modelling Methods and Frameworks

**Scope:** Literature and framework reconnaissance for the first New Zealand forecasting and static tax-policy slice. This is a research synthesis, not an empirical comparison of model performance. Framework documentation confirms capabilities, not that a model is valid for New Zealand or outperforms alternatives.

## Findings at a glance

- New Zealand already has a relevant structural macroeconomic precedent: the Reserve Bank's NZSIM is a small-open-economy structural model used in monetary-policy forecasting and analysis. Its existence is useful context, but does not provide this project with an executable/openly licensed model or establish that our proposed methods are accurate.[^nzsim]
- A minimal first forecast comparison should include a simple no-change or historical-mean benchmark, a univariate autoregressive/state-space model, and a small reduced-form VAR, all fitted and evaluated independently at the agreed 1-, 4-, and 8-quarter horizons. `statsmodels` implements these methods and forecast intervals; VAR use requires careful treatment of stationarity and lag choice.[^smtsa][^smvar][^smforecast]
- For short NZ quarterly samples, compare those transparent baselines before adding parameters. A BVAR is a useful subsequent candidate if a multivariate VAR is unstable or over-parameterized; its shrinkage priors are assumptions that require sensitivity checks.[^dynare]
- Equal weighting and performance-weighted averaging are sensible baselines to compare. Published forecast-combination research often finds equal weights difficult to beat because estimated weights add sampling error. Forecast errors and model outputs are often correlated; inverse-error weights alone do not account for that covariance.[^timmermann][^elliott][^weights]
- A forecast is a prediction about an observed outcome. A policy effect is a contrast between a policy outcome and a credible no-policy counterfactual. DSGE/CGE simulations can encode theory and counterfactual assumptions, but those assumptions and model closure are not empirical identification by themselves. Treat predictive validation and causal policy evaluation as separate registries.[^gtap][^causal]
- Start with models that produce the specified targets and can be tested against point-in-time NZ data. Defer a large DSGE/CGE, agent-based, system-dynamics, Gaussian-process or deep-learning build until a concrete use case and adequate data justify its cost and assumptions.

## Model-family comparison

Computational effort is relative and depends on model dimension, calibration, inference method, and implementation. “Data needed” describes the usual requirement; it is not a claim that all inputs are available for New Zealand.

| Family | Mathematical basis and usual inputs/outputs | Identification, horizons, computation, strengths and limitations | NZ fit, open implementations, integration and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DSGE** | Intertemporal household/firm optimization, market-clearing conditions, policy rules and stochastic shocks. Inputs include national accounts, prices, rates, labour data, priors or calibrated parameters. Outputs include forecast paths, shocks, impulse responses, welfare or policy counterfactuals. | Requires a specified structure, equilibrium conditions, shock processes, observation equations and parameter identification. Estimation and nonlinear solution can be costly; MCMC and higher-order solutions add computation. Medium/long-run paths and conditional policy scenarios are common uses. Economic discipline and explicit mechanisms are strengths; misspecification, parameter/prior sensitivity, omitted sectors and difficult identification limit inference. | NZSIM is an RBNZ small-open-economy structural model used in monetary-policy projections and analysis.[^nzsim][^nzhandbook] Dynare is open-source software for solving, estimating and forecasting DSGE models, including Bayesian estimation and BVAR/DSGE-VAR tools; its main workflow is MATLAB/Octave rather than native Python.[^dynare] Treat as a later benchmark/adapter, not the initial Python slice. RBNZ documentation is evidence of local institutional use, not a public out-of-sample score for this project. |
| **CGE** | A calibrated system of household, firm, government and trade behaviours is solved for prices and quantities that clear markets, given a benchmark social accounting/national-accounting dataset, elasticities, taxes and a closure. Outputs are scenario changes in output, prices, welfare, trade and factor returns. | Results depend on functional forms, elasticities, benchmark data, market structure, and “closure” rules such as labour-market or fiscal adjustment. Rich sector detail and economy-wide consistency can be valuable, but the solution is a conditional thought experiment; many elasticities are calibrated or literature-based. Large nonlinear systems can be computationally intensive. Often comparative-static or medium/long-run scenarios, not near-term forecasting. | GTAP documents a multiregion/multisector CGE with explicit assumptions and alternative closures, and notes that few CGE models have been tested as a whole against historical experience; results are sensitive to assumptions and base data.[^gtap] GEMPACK/GTAP are established implementations, with model and data access/licensing to verify separately. Potential later policy/scenario adapter if NZ input-output and fiscal data support the necessary calibration. |
| **Structural / semi-structural macroeconometric** | A system of equations links output gaps, inflation, interest rates, wages, exchange rates and expectations through theory-motivated relationships, estimated or calibrated from macro data. A semi-structural model often keeps reduced-form gap/trend equations instead of full microfoundations. Outputs are coherent quarterly paths, conditional scenarios and narratives. | Identification arises from restrictions, calibrated coefficients, priors and/or time-series estimation. Short samples and breaks weaken estimates; filtering potential output is uncertain. A quarterly medium-term forecast/policy horizon is a natural use. It can combine expert judgement and consistency, but imposed links may be misspecified and should not be confused with causal identification. | NZSIM supplies a directly relevant New Zealand precedent. IMF FPAS work describes small semi-structural quarterly models as practical, adaptable tools, while distinguishing them from fully microfounded DSGEs.[^nzsim][^imffpas][^finex] Python implementation would likely be a custom, typed model over `statsmodels` components; the reviewed RBNZ material does not establish reusable public code. Keep model parameters and structural assumptions visible. |
| **VAR / SVAR / BVAR** | Reduced-form VAR: \(y_t=c+\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_i y_{t-i}+u_t\). VAR predicts each series from lags of all series. SVAR imposes contemporaneous or long-run restrictions to recover structural shocks. BVAR places priors (often shrinkage) on coefficients. Inputs are aligned time series; outputs are multivariate forecasts, and optionally impulse responses/decompositions. | Reduced-form forecasting does not require causal identification, but stationarity/cointegration, lag specification, sample size and stability matter. SVAR causal interpretation requires credible identifying restrictions; recursive/Cholesky results depend on variable order.[^smvar][^svar] A BVAR prior can regularize a short/high-dimensional sample, but prior sensitivity remains. Low/moderate compute for small systems; increasing variables/lags raises parameter count. Useful for quarterly short/medium horizons. | `statsmodels` supplies VAR/VECM estimation, lag selection, forecast and analysis, and explicitly warns its standard VAR assumes stationary inputs.[^smvar] Dynare offers Bayesian VAR estimation/forecasting with Minnesota priors.[^dynare] A small reduced-form VAR is a good initial multivariate comparator, with no structural interpretation unless a separate identification design is specified. No NZ performance result is claimed here. |
| **State-space / Kalman filtering** | Latent state transition \(x_t=T_t x_{t-1}+\eta_t\), observation equation \(y_t=Z_t x_t+\epsilon_t\); Kalman filtering updates state distributions as observations arrive, and smoothing uses future observations. Models include local level/trend, seasonal components, dynamic factors and time-varying coefficients. | Requires meaningful state/observation equations and assumptions about noise distributions/covariances; latent states can be weakly identified. Linear Gaussian models are efficient; nonlinear/non-Gaussian variants may require approximate or particle filters. Filtering supports online updates and missing observations under model assumptions; smoothing is not point-in-time safe for historical real-time forecasts. Natural for short-to-medium time-series forecasts and measurement-error/state estimation. | `statsmodels` supports SARIMAX, unobserved components, dynamic factor and related state-space models, Kalman filtering/smoothing, simulation, prediction and intervals.[^smstate][^smforecast] Suitable for the first univariate autoregressive/state-space comparator. Keep filtered-at-origin states for backtests; never feed smoothed estimates that use future data into a historical forecast. |
| **Agent-based economic models (ABM)** | Heterogeneous agents (households, firms, banks, government) follow explicit behavioural rules and interact through networks/markets; aggregate outcomes emerge from simulation. Inputs include agent attributes, rules, networks, initial distributions and random seeds; outputs are distributions of trajectories/scenarios. | Identification is often weak: behaviours, interactions and initial heterogeneity must be measured or assumed. Calibration, sensitivity analysis and validation are essential; matching aggregate moments does not uniquely identify micro-rules. Repeated stochastic runs can be computationally expensive. Useful for nonlinear interactions, heterogeneity and mechanisms that representative-agent models omit, but not a default national forecast. | Mesa is an open Python ABM framework for agents, spaces, data collection, analysis and parameter sweeps.[^mesa] Do not instantiate individual NZ households/firms from aggregate statistics; use explicit synthetic populations only as labelled scenarios. For later use, validate micro rules against independent evidence and aggregate implications against held-out NZ data. |
| **Tax-benefit microsimulation** | Applies coded statutory tax/transfer rules to person/household records; static models compute liabilities and disposable income, while behavioural/dynamic extensions add take-up, labour supply or lifecycle responses. Inputs require detailed income, household composition, age and eligibility fields; outputs include individual and aggregate distributional/fiscal changes. | Static results identify mechanical effects under rules, not behavioural response. Aggregate precision depends on representative microdata, weights, non-take-up, record coverage and policy-rule fidelity. Behavioural elasticities require separate evidence and add identification uncertainty. Static computation is usually modest; population-scale simulation is manageable but data access/governance is often the constraint. | OpenFisca is an open-source rule engine and documents itself as a static microsimulation system; reforms calculate rule-based effects but do not automatically model behavioural consumption response.[^openfisca][^reforms] It is a candidate only after verifying NZ law coverage, licence and data availability. First implementation can be an exact deterministic calculator for supplied inputs; aggregate costing must use verifiable public population distributions or report unavailable. |
| **Input-output (IO)** | Fixed-coefficient production relationships: \(x=Ax+f\), so gross output \(x=(I-A)^{-1}f\), where A is input coefficients and f final demand. Supply-use/input-output accounts map product and industry transactions; outputs include direct/indirect production, value-added and import requirements. | Assumes proportional input recipes, usually fixed prices and no substitution, capacity response or endogenous behavioural change. It is an accounting/structural linkage tool rather than a causal policy model. Computation is typically modest linear algebra; detail is limited by table granularity. Static or comparative demand shocks are the common use. | OECD IOTs provide inter-industry flows and link to value-added components; UNSD describes SUTs as a coherent national-accounts framework integrating production, income and expenditure GDP.[^oecdio][^unsut] Use NZ supply-use tables later for accounting consistency and sector exposure. Do not infer dynamic multipliers or behavioural effects from Leontief multipliers alone. |
| **System dynamics** | Stocks accumulate flows (differential/difference equations); feedback loops, delays, nonlinear relations and lookup functions generate trajectories. Inputs are system boundaries, causal structure, initial stocks, rates and parameters; outputs are scenario paths and sensitivity results. | Causal loop diagrams do not identify parameters or policy effects. Equations and feedback structure are often elicited from theory/experts; calibration and extreme-condition tests are necessary. Time horizons depend on the stocks and delays; computation is usually moderate but sensitivity/Monte Carlo sweeps can grow. Strong for feedback narratives; risky if feedback equations are not empirically grounded. | PySD translates Vensim/Stella-style models into Python for execution and analysis; the JOSS paper describes stock/flow and feedback concepts.[^pysd] Could later document long-run feedback mechanisms, but should not replace forecast validation or a causal design. |
| **Bayesian hierarchical models** | Group parameters are drawn from shared population distributions, e.g. \(\theta_j\sim N(\mu,\tau^2)\), enabling partial pooling across groups/countries/regions; priors and likelihood combine to produce posterior distributions. | Requires exchangeability or a justified hierarchy, prior specification and data compatibility. Partial pooling trades variance against bias and does not make structurally different countries interchangeable. Posterior inference can be demanding (MCMC/diagnostics); hierarchical scope and subgroup sample size matter. Useful when several comparable regions/countries exist, not as a substitute for NZ evidence. | PyMC and NumPyro support general Bayesian modelling; NumPyro uses JAX and demonstrates hierarchical inference and MCMC, with computational benefits dependent on model and hardware.[^numpyro] Later international transfer should use NZ-specific effects and held-out NZ evaluation, with a NZ-only comparator and an explicit rejection rule. |
| **Gaussian processes (GP)** | A prior over functions \(f\sim GP(m,k)\) encodes smoothness/periodicity/other structure via covariance kernel k; conditioning on observations gives a predictive distribution. Inputs are features/time and target observations; outputs are nonlinear forecasts with posterior variance. | Kernel choice is a substantive prior about temporal smoothness and scale; data do not identify arbitrary extrapolation. Standard dense GP regression has cubic time/quadratic memory in sample count; sparse approximations reduce cost but add approximation assumptions. Often useful for small smooth datasets/interpolation, but long-horizon macro extrapolation and structural breaks are concerns. | scikit-learn's `GaussianProcessRegressor` returns predictive mean/standard deviation and describes kernel/noise assumptions and high-dimensional limitations; PyMC and NumPyro also expose GP implementations.[^skgp][^pymcgp][^numpyro] A later experimental inference method, not an initial macro benchmark without demonstrated value beyond simpler models. |
| **Causal inference / econometric policy evaluation** | Identifies a treatment-effect estimand by comparing potential outcomes under treatment and a counterfactual. Designs include DiD, synthetic control, RD, IV and experiments; inputs depend on design and outputs are causal estimates with uncertainty. | Each design needs its own identification assumptions: e.g., parallel untreated trends/no anticipation/no spillovers for DiD; credible donor fit for synthetic control; continuity/no manipulation near an RD cutoff; relevance, independence and exclusion for IV.[^did][^scm][^rd][^iv] These assumptions are not guaranteed by a forecasting model. Compute ranges from simple regression to intensive design-specific estimation; horizons depend on outcome timing and design. | `linearmodels` supports panel, IV, GMM and system regressions; `statsmodels` supports time-series econometrics.[^linearmodels][^smtsa] Use only when a suitable NZ policy, treatment/comparison units, outcome data and assumptions exist. Policy simulation without a credible no-policy counterfactual is not causal validation. |
| **Machine-learning forecasting** | Learns a predictive function \(\hat y_{t+h}=f(X_t)\) from lagged features, exogenous predictors, regularized regressions, trees/boosting or neural networks; outputs point or probabilistic predictions depending on estimator. | Requires careful feature availability at each forecast date and time-ordered validation. Flexible learners can overfit small quarterly samples, leak future information, and extrapolate implausibly; interpretation is not causal identification. Compute spans low for regularized regression/trees to high for deep models/tuning. Can capture nonlinearities with enough independent data, but macro samples are often short and subject to breaks. | scikit-learn includes gradient boosting, random forests, stacking, and probabilistic calibration tools; LightGBM and PyTorch are other options requested for later assessment.[^sklearn] Begin only after expanding the information set and comparing strictly with the same rolling-origin baseline. |

## Candidate first-slice model set

The following is a research recommendation, not a claim that these models are optimal for New Zealand. The data catalogue and validation work must first confirm target definitions, usable sample length, transformations, release timing and revisions.

1. **Simple benchmark:** last value/no-change or a training-window mean, chosen per target definition and reported alongside all methods. A benchmark reveals whether complexity adds predictive value.
2. **Univariate state-space autoregression:** one SARIMAX/AR or local-level/trend specification per target, fitted independently. It is transparent, parsimonious and can return forecast intervals; compare a small predeclared specification set rather than selecting on the final test window.[^smforecast]
3. **Small multivariate VAR:** model the three agreed target series jointly, using lag selection only inside each training window and transformations selected from NZ data diagnostics. It tests whether cross-series dynamics add forecast value. If parameter instability or short-sample estimation makes this unreliable, research a Minnesota-prior BVAR as the next model rather than silently shrinking/regularizing the VAR.[^smvar][^dynare]

Evaluate each at 1, 4 and 8 quarters from identical forecast origins, with identical information sets and target vintages. Use the same baseline, transformations, missing-data policy and refitting cadence across model comparisons wherever technically meaningful. SARIMAX and VAR both use lagged macro data, so their errors are likely dependent; do not count agreement as independent evidence.

For ensembles, compare equal weights with a transparent performance-weighted rule using only forecast errors observed before each origin. A basic inverse-MSE weighting rule is a benchmark, not a covariance-optimal method; it can be noisy in short windows and ignores cross-model error covariance. Report model error correlations by target and horizon. Keep model-level predictive distributions, where available, and calibrate ensemble intervals separately; a weighted point forecast plus an in-sample RMSE is not a calibrated probability distribution.[^timmermann][^elliott][^weights]

## Validation evidence and boundaries

- The RBNZ's published NZSIM work documents a structural model for New Zealand and its role in forecasting and policy analysis. The retrieved description says the model was evaluated for its ability to explain NZ data; that is not equivalent to a rolling-origin predictive score or independent causal-policy validation.[^nzsim]
- The IMF review of forecasting/policy-analysis systems reports that many institutions begin with a relatively simple semi-structural model and cautions that a full DSGE need not improve forecast performance or policy support. This is institutional experience, not a general ranking of methods.[^imffpas]
- GTAP's own model overview acknowledges that few CGE models have been tested as a whole against historical experience, and emphasizes calibration/closure sensitivity. Interpret CGE outputs as conditional scenarios unless separately tested.[^gtap]
- The sources reviewed here do not establish a quantitative head-to-head result for the proposed NZ targets, horizons, vintages, or first-slice implementations. No forecast score is asserted. That result must come from the project's point-in-time, rolling-origin evaluation.
- A forecast test evaluates prediction of realized economic variables. A causal test evaluates the estimated policy-minus-counterfactual effect against an independent design-based estimate. Reserve each for its own benchmark, assumptions and scoring protocol.[^causal]

## Framework reference table

| Tool/framework | Relevant capabilities | Fit and caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [statsmodels](https://www.statsmodels.org/stable/tsa.html) | AR/ARIMA, VAR/VECM, state-space/Kalman, forecast intervals, diagnostics | Best initial workhorse for transparent quarterly time-series comparators. |
| [Dynare](https://www.dynare.org/manual/) | DSGE solving, estimation, forecasting, Bayesian VAR and DSGE-VAR | Mature model-specific system; MATLAB/Octave workflow and model-specific data/parameters. |
| [OpenFisca](https://openfisca.org/doc/) | Rule-based tax/benefit calculations and static reforms | Candidate for the static tax domain after NZ law coverage and aggregate-data access are verified. |
| [PyMC](https://www.pymc.io/projects/docs/en/stable/) | Bayesian hierarchical models, GP and probabilistic inference | Flexible, but posterior diagnostics and compute are model responsibilities. |
| [NumPyro](https://num.pyro.ai/en/stable/) / [JAX](https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/) | Bayesian inference, NUTS/VI, differentiable computation and accelerator execution | Useful for custom Bayesian/inference work; added functional/JAX complexity is unnecessary for first baselines. |
| [linearmodels](https://bashtage.github.io/linearmodels/) | Panel estimators, IV/2SLS, GMM and system regression | Useful once a defensible causal/panel design and data exist; software does not verify identifying assumptions. |
| [Mesa](https://mesa.readthedocs.io/stable/) | Python ABM agents, spaces, collection, batch runs and visualization | Useful for a later heterogeneous-agent prototype, not a near-term macro forecast without validated behaviour/data. |
| [scikit-learn](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/user_guide.html) | Supervised ML, GPs, tree ensembles, stacking and calibration | Candidate for later benchmarks; strict time-series feature/vintage controls required. |
| [PySD](https://pysd.readthedocs.io/en/stable/) | Execute system-dynamics models in Python | Helps bring stock-flow models into Python analysis; model structure and coefficients still need validation. |
| [GEMPACK / GTAP](https://www.gtap.agecon.purdue.edu/models/) | CGE equations, policy scenario analysis and trade-sector modelling | Relevant to future sectoral/fiscal scenarios; check code/database licence and calibration data before adoption. |
